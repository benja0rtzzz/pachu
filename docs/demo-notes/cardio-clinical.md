# Cardiology — Clinical Reference

## Atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained arrhythmia. Disorganized atrial
electrical activity replaces coordinated contraction; the ventricular response is
characteristically irregularly irregular. ECG shows absent P waves, a fibrillatory
baseline, and irregular RR intervals.

Stroke risk is stratified by CHA2DS2-VASc; anticoagulation is indicated at a score of
2 or greater in men and 3 or greater in women. Direct oral anticoagulants are
preferred over warfarin in non-valvular disease. Rate control with a beta-blocker or
non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker is first-line for stable patients;
rhythm-control strategy is reserved for symptomatic patients despite adequate rate
control.

## Heart failure

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is defined as an ejection fraction at or
below 40 percent. Guideline-directed medical therapy comprises an angiotensin
receptor-neprilysin inhibitor, a beta-blocker with mortality benefit (carvedilol,
bisoprolol, or metoprolol succinate), a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, and an
SGLT2 inhibitor. Loop diuretics are titrated for volume status and do not modify
mortality.

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction is characterized by an ejection
fraction of 50 percent or higher with clinical signs of congestion. SGLT2 inhibitors
have demonstrated mortality and hospitalization benefit and are now first-line.

## Acute coronary syndromes

ST-elevation myocardial infarction requires immediate reperfusion. Primary
percutaneous coronary intervention is the preferred strategy when achievable within
90 minutes of first medical contact. Fibrinolysis is acceptable when timely
intervention is unavailable.

Non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome encompasses unstable angina and
non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction. Management is guided by risk stratification —
TIMI or GRACE — and includes dual antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, and an
early invasive strategy in high-risk patients.

Troponin elevation is sensitive but not specific for myocardial infarction; alternative
causes include myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, and renal failure. The
universal definition of myocardial infarction requires troponin elevation with at
least one finding of ischemia: symptoms, ECG changes, imaging evidence of new wall
motion abnormality, or angiographic thrombus.

## Hypertension

Stage 1 hypertension is defined by office blood pressure of 130 to 139 systolic or 80
to 89 diastolic. Stage 2 begins at 140 systolic or 90 diastolic. Pharmacologic
therapy is initiated at stage 2, or at stage 1 with cardiovascular risk above 10
percent.

First-line agents are thiazide diuretics, dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers,
ACE inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers. Beta-blockers are not first-line
absent a compelling indication such as heart failure or post-infarct status.

Resistant hypertension — uncontrolled despite three agents at maximum tolerated doses
including a diuretic — warrants evaluation for secondary causes: primary
aldosteronism, renal artery stenosis, obstructive sleep apnea, and pheochromocytoma.

## ECG interpretation

Systematic interpretation proceeds through rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, and
morphology. Normal sinus rhythm requires a rate of 60 to 100, a P wave preceding every
QRS, and a PR interval between 120 and 200 milliseconds.

Left ventricular hypertrophy by Sokolow-Lyon criteria is met when the sum of the S
wave in V1 and the R wave in V5 or V6 exceeds 35 millimeters. Bundle branch block
prolongs QRS to 120 milliseconds or more; a left bundle morphology features broad
monophasic R waves in lateral leads.

ST elevation must be evaluated against the J point in two contiguous leads. Diffuse
ST elevation with PR depression is more consistent with pericarditis than with
infarction.

## Pharmacology essentials

ACE inhibitors reduce mortality in heart failure and post-infarct, and slow
progression of diabetic nephropathy. The principal adverse effect is dry cough,
mediated by bradykinin accumulation; angiotensin receptor blockers are interchangeable
when cough develops.

Beta-blockers blunt sympathetic drive at the beta-1 receptor, lowering heart rate,
contractility, and conduction velocity. Carvedilol and labetalol additionally
antagonize alpha-1 receptors, contributing to vasodilation. Cardioselective agents
(metoprolol, bisoprolol, atenolol) are preferred in patients with reactive airway
disease.

Statins lower LDL cholesterol via inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase. Mortality benefit
is established in secondary prevention and in primary prevention for patients at
elevated cardiovascular risk. The principal adverse effects are myopathy and, rarely,
hepatotoxicity.

Anticoagulants must be matched to indication. Direct oral anticoagulants are preferred
in non-valvular atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism. Warfarin remains
indicated for mechanical valves and rheumatic mitral stenosis with atrial
fibrillation.
