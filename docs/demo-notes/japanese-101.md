# Japanese 101 — Week 3 Notes

So I finally figured out the difference between the writing systems. Here's what's
clicking for me.

## The three scripts

Japanese uses three scripts at the same time, which sounds horrible but is actually fine
once you get used to it. Hiragana is the rounded one, used for grammar bits and Japanese
words. Katakana is the angular one, used mostly for foreign loanwords like "コーヒー"
(coffee). Kanji are the Chinese characters, used for nouns and the stems of verbs.

You'll see all three in a single sentence. That's normal.

## Greetings

Konnichiwa is the daytime hello, around midday to early evening. Ohayou is the casual
"morning" — add gozaimasu to make it polite. Konbanwa is for evenings. Sayonara is
goodbye, but actually nobody really uses it for short partings; mata ne ("see you") is
way more common between friends.

Arigatou means thanks, and arigatou gozaimasu is the polite version. If someone thanks
you, you can say douitashimashite, which is a mouthful but means "you're welcome."

## Particles

This is the part that confused me for a week. Particles are tiny words that mark what
role a noun is playing in a sentence.

- **wa** marks the topic ("as for X..."). Written は but pronounced "wa" in this case.
- **ga** marks the subject. The difference between wa and ga is subtle and I'm still
  getting it wrong half the time.
- **wo** marks the direct object. Written を but pronounced "o".
- **ni** marks a destination or indirect object, like "to" in English.
- **de** marks the location where an action happens.

Example: Watashi wa sushi wo tabemasu. "As for me, I eat sushi."

## Verbs and the masu form

Japanese verbs come at the end of the sentence, which still feels backwards. The polite
present-tense form ends in -masu. So taberu (to eat, dictionary form) becomes tabemasu
(eats / will eat, polite). Past tense is -mashita: tabemashita ("ate").

The negative is -masen ("does not eat" = tabemasen) and the negative past is -masen
deshita.

There's also the te-form, which is used for stitching things together — like "and then"
or making requests. Tabete kudasai means "please eat." We haven't covered te-form
properly yet, but I keep seeing it in reading homework.

## Numbers

Counting in Japanese is wild because the words change based on what you're counting.
Ichi, ni, san, shi (or yon), go, roku, shichi (or nana), hachi, kyuu, juu — that's one
through ten in the abstract sense. But for counting flat objects, long objects, people,
or animals, the counter word changes. Hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu is the "general"
counter for things up to ten, and it's a safe fallback when you don't know the
specific counter.

## Days of the week

The days are named after planets / classical elements, same as in English roughly:

- Getsuyoubi = Monday (moon day)
- Kayoubi = Tuesday (fire day)
- Suiyoubi = Wednesday (water day)
- Mokuyoubi = Thursday (wood day)
- Kinyoubi = Friday (gold day)
- Doyoubi = Saturday (earth day)
- Nichiyoubi = Sunday (sun day)

The pattern made it click for me — once you know the kanji for the elements, the days
basically read themselves.

## Useful phrases

Sumimasen is one of those words that does everything. It's "excuse me" when you bump
into someone, "I'm sorry" for small apologies, and "thanks" when someone holds a door for
you. Probably the single most useful word I've learned.

Wakarimasen means "I don't understand," which is going to be my constant companion.
Wakarimasu is "I understand," which is more aspirational.

Hai is yes, iie is no, but in real conversation people use a lot of softer affirmations
like sou desu ne ("that's right, isn't it") instead of a flat hai.

context-dependent and tied to social hierarchy in ways I'm not catching yet). And kanji
readings — every kanji has like four pronunciations and you pick based on context.
