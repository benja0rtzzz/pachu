# Cold War — Survey Notes

## Origins

The Cold War emerged in the years after the Second World War, as the wartime alliance
between the United States and the Soviet Union dissolved into mutual suspicion. Two
events in 1947 are usually treated as the formal opening: the announcement of the
Truman Doctrine in March, which committed the United States to containing communism,
and the Marshall Plan in June, which offered economic aid to rebuild Western Europe.

The Iron Curtain, a phrase popularized by Winston Churchill in his 1946 Fulton speech,
described the political and ideological boundary that divided Europe into a Western
sphere aligned with the United States and an Eastern sphere dominated by the Soviet
Union.

## Containment

Containment, articulated most clearly by George Kennan in his 1947 Long Telegram,
became the guiding strategy of American foreign policy. The doctrine sought to prevent
the geographic expansion of Soviet influence rather than to roll it back.

NATO was founded in 1949 as the principal military expression of containment in
Europe. Its founding members included the United States, the United Kingdom, France,
Canada, and several smaller Western European states. The Soviet Union responded by
establishing the Warsaw Pact in 1955.

## Berlin

Berlin was the most volatile flashpoint of the early Cold War. The Berlin Blockade of
1948 to 1949, in which the Soviet Union sealed off ground access to West Berlin, was
defeated by the Berlin Airlift, an Allied operation that supplied the city by air for
nearly a year.

The Berlin Wall, erected in 1961 to halt East German emigration to the West, became
the most visible symbol of the Cold War. It would stand until 1989.

## The Cuban Missile Crisis

In October 1962, American reconnaissance aircraft photographed Soviet nuclear missiles
in Cuba. The ensuing thirteen-day standoff between the Kennedy and Khrushchev
administrations is widely considered the moment the Cold War came closest to a hot
nuclear exchange. Resolution came through a public Soviet withdrawal of the Cuban
missiles and a quiet American agreement to remove Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

## Detente and the SALT Treaties

The late 1960s and 1970s saw a partial relaxation of tensions, known as detente. The
Strategic Arms Limitation Talks produced two principal agreements: SALT I in 1972 and
SALT II in 1979. SALT I capped the number of strategic nuclear delivery vehicles each
side could maintain; SALT II added limits on multiple independently targetable reentry
vehicles.

The Helsinki Accords, signed in 1975, traded Western recognition of post-war European
borders for Soviet commitments on human rights — commitments that, paradoxically,
would become a tool used by Eastern bloc dissident movements.

## The End

Mikhail Gorbachev's accession to General Secretary in 1985 marked the beginning of the
end. His policies of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) loosened
internal control over the Soviet bloc. The peaceful revolutions of 1989 swept away
communist governments across Eastern Europe; the Berlin Wall fell in November of that
year. The Soviet Union itself dissolved on December 26, 1991.

The Cold War is conventionally dated as ending with the dissolution of the Soviet
Union, though scholars disagree on whether the Malta Summit in December 1989, the fall
of the Berlin Wall, or the Soviet collapse marks the true terminus.

## Key concepts to remember

Bipolarity describes the international system that emerged after 1945, structured
around the two superpowers and their respective alliance systems. Mutually assured
destruction, or MAD, refers to the doctrine that a full-scale nuclear exchange would
destroy both attackers, deterring either side from initiating one. Proxy war refers
to armed conflicts in third countries — Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan — fought
between client states or factions of the two superpowers without direct
superpower-on-superpower combat.
